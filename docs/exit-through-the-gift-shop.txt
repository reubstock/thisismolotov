Exit through the Gift Shop
  
                 
Douglas,


I'm not impressed by six hexagons, an AI book, or a Master Plan.


A co-founder's job is to build a team and move it toward a goal. In seven months I heard too few “Yes”s and saw too little movement. 


Then you unveiled the Master Plan.


Months earlier, I remember your face lighting up after I learned Claude to build the prototype of what we'd been discussing. Every time I made something new, you latched on and gave it a new Hollywood name — “GoFundMe for the Planet”, the “Peace Corps for the Planet” and “No Kings for the Environment”.


YES isn't an entity yet. It's a subset of the Lexicon with no equity. I was not paid. When two people work together without a contract, on one person's IP, the other's contributions have a name. Gifts.


You pressed me to choose a title inside a shifting, unfunded scope, and in the six weeks since you started calling the Lexicon our “financial sponsor” you've avoided compensation and every other term. You never described the board that could fire me. Who's on it besides you and your wife?


So I asked myself: how could I have seen this coming?


From the start I've navigated a minefield of your false dualities — "fix the world or fix yourself," "nouns or verbs." That thinking may make you feel effective, but I find it exclusionary and, by definition, fractional. It hides what is usually the only true answer. Both.


Your Theory of Change describes a locally rooted movement. I've studied these. You don’t reboot your operating system with your zipcode and an iPhone, somewhere in the birth canal of a museum a thousand miles from where you live.


That's stagecraft — the Holocaust Museum's elevator, beautifully done.  Stagecraft isn't what keeps a movement alive. Movements live on sustained involvement, social inspiration, and accountability for what people contribute. The biggest ingredient I know of is a format for people to share how extraction has harmed them and how they'll enact restoration.


You've scorned groups since the start, sneering at Philip's "club for rich guys with meaningless lives" and the Petaluma philosophers. I never told you this but ten weeks into their meetings, one of Philip's members had a near-fatal accident. The group shared a photo of themselves, previously strangers, crowded into his hospital room. That has nothing to do with self versus whole, or rich versus poor. 


Those people built something worth showing up for, and if that's in your architecture, you can do anything.


It's fine that you're a solo hero surrounded by good people of action. But you keep using my word, for a reason.  Movement.


You, Greta, and all the Gateses have come up short, and the answer is the very thing you disdain. It isn't about saying YES. You're looking, quite literally, through the wrong end of the telescope. That's not a taunt or a critique of where your plan stands. It's a fact, and I so badly wanted to design it with you. As always, it's a two-man job.
.
Most movements are against something — racism, ruin, addiction. The more natural framing isn't YES, it's against Extraction, taught alongside Restoration, two sides of one coin. Until people understand the cause, they're powerless on the cure.


In your world, movements are rare.  To me they're rare because the field decided letters, lawsuits, grants and donations have the better ROI, and because movements are messy in a way no Allen AI dataset ever is. They're messy because they're people, teaching people, doing meaningful things where they live and sharing stories larger than their own.


They may be sparked by a video or a tool, but they're about people, and they can't be built by someone who thinks he's above them or derides "reading from a text."
You've lectured me on Rachel Carson for weeks, so you must know why Silent Spring worked. The title, the rigor, the readers who felt betrayed and invaded by an invisible force. What those readers did next was the movement. Silent Spring was only its text.


Here's what I need you to hear. In seven months I wrangled you and Josh into a sellable idea. I read your book, sat through the African mangoes and the Peruvian potatoes, and drafted, shaped and delivered the thing. I don't take credit for much, but I showed up — not as a "groups guy," but as your co-founder, your architect, and your friend.


Open the group documents. You'll find draft after draft asking you the same question: how do the groups relate to the programming, the schedules, Restore6, the actions? You never answered, and I understand why. 


We hadn't figured it out. That's fine. I'd no more ask you to flesh out the season schedule than expect you to call me on the carpet for groups. They're intertwined and unfinished. Making things has never cost me much time. Unresolved dependencies do.


Which is why your evasion felt like sabotage. Don't want me as a co-founder? Fine. Blame it on me? Not fine.


Groups are hard because they have to be original and right. It takes an environmental expert and a movement expert talking to each other with respect.


You said the book would take years. The AA Big Book( 37M copies ) is 164 pages plus recovery stories, written by one person, shared chapter by chapter with the early groups, announced in the Saturday Evening Post in 1939, and credited to a hundred authors by the time it was printed. Written by many, changing the wheels on a moving bus. That's the spec.


The fact is you cut me out, and when I asked why, you wouldn't even admit you'd done it. You told me to tattoo on my arm that groups were in. Your plan uses the word four times, never about building a movement. In their place, you put a museum.


For a while I thought it was a misunderstanding. I now know you did it on purpose, and that if it happened once it would happen again. It was a way to push me out and instead of one "mea culpa," you gave me wordplay.


Tuesday was the clue. You said you'd price groups to a funder at $20K. If that's real, you’d sat on it for seven months. If it isn't, you said it to force my hand. Both are worse than the truth. If distribution is the problem, you're long on hexagons and short on the only thing that distributes: people, organized locally. I wonder what $20K buys you. Not the answer to your own question.


You'll raise money anyway. Even as a website, the idea’s OK.  You know useful people, you're good in a room. What I can't get past is that you ridicule groups, the one thing every movement is built from. Deferring to other organizations is a clever stutter step — a hundred short videos and actions in a year — but it isn't the movement I've heard about for seven months.


You cut 58%  of the plan, made me read it and then asked "Who, me?".  I read your 43 pages line by line and asked “what replaced groups?”. Your museum did, 1:1 -  something neither Josh nor I signed on for and that wasn’t in any of the 20 decks we built.


I looked for a long time for the word for what you did to me over 7 months, 30 calls, 50 documents. Not disassembly - I think you’re sincere. Not using a collaborator. Maybe it was a mistake. Then I realized it had been hanging over the whole thing the entire time, the sign above a museum that still doesn't exist.


Extraction.


Maybe it's time to pause your judgement of  the talkers and noun-people and sit quiet with the irony in your unchangeable creation myth.


Restoratively yours,


Reuben
