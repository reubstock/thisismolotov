1. Executive Summary


The YES Movement is a nonprofit platform that channels collective intelligence into environmental action at the local level. It gives environmental organizations, activists, and the general public the tools to measure ecological impact, share what works, and convert abstract concern into specific local action.


1.1 The Problem




Popular movements often fail for predictable reasons. Large crowds gather. Handpainted signs are waved. Proclamations are made. But public outcry arrives faster than local organizations can channel it into sustained work on the ground. The next steps never materialize. Momentum fades. Outrage subsides. Meanwhile, climate communication makes another persistent mistake: it traffics in distant catastrophe (climate crisis pornography) rather than local possibility (a pathway to action that starts where you live).


The result? People are motivated yet find nowhere to direct their energy. It’s not a finding the solution problem. In most cases, solutions exist. It’s a distribution of the solution problem.


Today, thousands of small environmental organizations work in isolation. They lack common tools to express their impacts, share knowledge, or cost-effectively attract new members.


1.2 The Solution: Interlocking Components




The YES Movement is a solutions platform for the environmental movement. It offers the antidote for our addiction to extraction by borrowing from the architecture of movements that endure. Civil Rights, Alcoholics Anonymous, and the broader Environmental Movement itself share a common structure: a founding text, distributed and largely leaderless governance, and reliance on members for labor, decision-making, and funding. The YES Movement supplies all three. RestoreSix provides the common language and impact visualization framework that connects the movement. The YES Online Platform provides its core infrastructure, connecting people to local organizations, actions, tools, and documented impacts. The book, Substack and YouTube channels, changemakers, and the Museum of Extraction all serve as acquisition and activation engines, creating encounters that lead people to the platform. Local environmental organizations provide the opportunities to act and turn participation into measurable restoration.


Four interlocking components form this ecosystem:


* Molotov Alva and the Museum of Extraction (Book): a limited-release, both founding text and fundraising instrument
* The YES Online Platform: Impact Measurement Tools, an Action Map, a Field Guide, and a resource library built on RestoreSix
* Substack and YouTube Channel: weekly live programming, podcasts, short films, and social media shareables that build community and generate a vast knowledge library
* The Museum of Extraction Exhibit: an immersive physical experience, in tentpole and traveling formats, that converts visitors into activated local participants


Together, these components form a continuous pathway. Stories and experiences create encounters. The platform localizes those encounters and connects people to organizations. These organizations convert participation into action, then RestoreSix makes the resulting impacts visible, helping solutions scale.

1.4 Not a New Idea, Just One That’s Bigger and Better
The YES Movement is a new initiative. It brings together activist organizations, technology providers, and domain experts to forge an entirely new approach to environmental action. Yet it’s based on core components that have been developed over ten years by The Lexicon, field-tested, and embraced by hundreds of key stakeholders working at multilateral development banks, foundations, international organizations, corporations, and family offices.


The Lexicon is highly skilled in mobilization and movement building. It created Reawakened, the first global collective action to safeguard agrobiodiversity, bringing together hundreds of organizations. The work crystallized at the 2nd International Congress on Agrobiodiversity in Kunming, China, where its Ten Principles for Agrobiodiversity were ratified by a delegation of international experts. 


This work led to Can You Change the Future?, an initiative funded by Google. It brought together 1,000 companies, UN agencies, and NGOs under NDA to develop innovative solutions across fields ranging from Food is Medicine and regenerative agriculture to single-use plastics and dietary guidelines. That work resulted in new nonprofits, open-source tools for sustainable food sourcing, and the foundation that eventually became RestoreSix.


The YES Movement builds on this expertise. The Lexicon already knows how to convene organizations around a shared framework, turn complex issues into consensus-led programs, and move large networks from conversation to collective action. YES applies that model at a far greater scale, connecting organizations, changemakers, and the public in a single system designed to convert attention into participation.

Year 4, which focuses on horizontal scaling, satellite exhibit deployment, and expansion of AI Advisory tools, will be financed through a combination of follow-on philanthropy and earned revenue. 2. Theory of Change


People are more likely to act when they can connect a global crisis to a place they know and a community they care about. Or an organization they trust. They also want their contributions to matter. They want to see and understand the impact of their actions. The YES Movement transforms a broad environmental narrative into a pathway for local participation. 


Extraction is an addiction, and the YES Movement offers a prescription for the cure. 


At its center is RestoreSix, a framework for understanding ecological restoration across six interconnected domains: air, water, soil, biodiversity, equity, and climate. RestoreSix allows actions that may appear unrelated—a river cleanup, a community solar project, a pollinator garden, a food-rescue program, a soil-restoration initiative, or an air-quality campaign—to be understood as contributions to the same larger challenge of planetary restoration. It shows people that their actions matter and that, together, those actions can become a force for restoration.


2.1 Intended Outcomes


In the near term, the YES Movement gives people a practical way to identify their priorities and discover local organizations, then take part in tangible work. It also helps organizations utilize valuable tools to visualize their impacts. 


In the medium term, the movement will increase membership, volunteerism, donations, partnerships, and participation in local restoration efforts. It also helps organizations not only  document their actions but share the formula for success with others, allowing good ideas to scale.


In the long term, the YES Movement will support a distributed global culture of restoration. Thousands of locally rooted organizations and millions of people will join local initiatives while seeing themselves as part of one interconnected movement. The movement’s success will be measured by the number of organizations it impacts and the actions that scale. 


The ultimate change is cultural as well as ecological. The Movement seeks to replace helplessness with agency, isolation with community, fragmented environmental action with shared purpose, and the logic of extraction with a commitment to restoration. It asks people not simply to oppose what is wrong, but to participate in building what comes next—and to answer that invitation with a single word: Yes.

3. The Power of YES 






There is no elite group in a windowless bunker solving this. 
The cavalry isn't coming. 
What if it all started with you?




3.1 We look through the wrong end of the telescope. 


Seeing a tree is not the same as seeing a forest. The tools we use to find solutions only show us what we want to see, while blinding us to everything outside the frame. The result is a misdiagnosis that leads to a cure for the wrong ailment. Turn the telescope around, and solutions come into view that were there all along.


3.2  We think in fractions. Nature thinks in wholes.


For 12,000 years, we have perfected extraction, charging the cost of human progress to an unseen credit card. The first bills have come due. Climate disruption. Biodiversity loss. Water scarcity. Soil erosion. Polluted air. Mass displacement. These are not separate problems but expressions of a breakdown in a single, interconnected system. When we respond with fractional solutions, we should not be surprised when they produce only fractional change. Nature doesn't solve problems in isolation. It operates holistically. We should too.


3.3 Doing less bad is bad. We should do more good.


The frameworks we use to solve our problems are built around reduction. Reduce emissions. Reduce waste. Reduce harm. For two decades, experts have handed us planetary boundaries and accounting systems for the true cost of extraction. These mitigation models are well-intentioned but fail to account for human nature. Nobody gets out of bed in the morning to do less bad. But ask us to save the planet, and we show up. What if we stopped organizing against things and started building toward what we want, together?


3.4 We say No instead of YES. 


When we say No, we find perfectly good reasons not to act. We settle for incremental steps when transformative leaps are needed. What if we understood our fear of failure differently? What if failure wasn’t an endpoint? What if it offered an opportunity to pivot and stay on the path? What if we were for things instead of against them? What if saying YES turned our fear of failure into an invitation to act?


3.5 We follow our own paths. What if we shared a common destination?


Everyone wants their path to be unique, but uniqueness without alignment leads us nowhere. What if our paths led to a shared destination? The knowledge exists. The passion exists. What is missing is the infrastructure to scale success, helping people find solutions and apply them in their own communities. YES is that infrastructure.

4. RestoreSix


4.1 Thy Enemy Is Apathy


Most people have already reached their conclusions about climate chaos and their own responsibility to avert the crisis. Fatigue has set in. Apathy reigns. We either tune out the changes rapidly unfolding around us or brace ourselves for survival.


The planet needs a new story, yet awareness alone does not produce action. People understand that these crises connect, but understanding a problem is not the same as participating in its solution. Most people can’t see where to begin. They might still care, but they no longer believe their contribution matters.


That is a failure of infrastructure, not information. The YES Movement gives people a new way in: stories, exhibitions, books, films, digital content, public programs, and place-based experiences that lead to solutions they can apply where they live. The audience becomes participants, contributing to ecological restoration at a local level.
4.2 RestoreSix 


Over a five-year period with Google, The Lexicon gathered experts from more than 1,000 organizations, in domain areas ranging from regenerative agriculture to food as medicine. Each participant was asked a simple question: Can you change the future? Their answers converged on six key areas: air, water, soil, biodiversity, equity, and climate. The name for this framework? RestoreSix.


Environmental organizations are often asked to report their impacts. Developing accurate impact visualization tools can be costly and complex, putting them out of reach for all except the most well endowed organizations. The result is proprietary tools and systems. Without data interoperability and easy-to-read impact visualization tools, a creek cleanup in Portland and a native seed planting in Sacramento are wholly incommensurable. When neither organization can properly benchmark its activities, funders with catalytic capital have nothing to compare. The part is indiscernible from the whole. 


Building better software is only part of the solution. It also requires convening enough organizations to agree on standards and definitions, then aligning them on shared goals. RestoreSix is a system of attestations that makes the positive ecological impacts of an organization’s actions visible. It provides a common framework for visualizing both quantitative and qualitative impacts. It's used by multilateral development banks, international organizations, and agricultural research centers on six continents. It also features a peer-reviewed taxonomy and impact visualization tools built and tested at institutional scale.

It supports activities that create positive impacts, unlocking the full spectrum of ecological and social benefits: air, water, soil, biodiversity, equity, and climate. RestoreSix: One Local Action, Many Benefits


  
  
  



Green Heart Louisville planted 8,000+ trees and shrubs across four square miles of South Louisville, in neighborhoods with the least canopy in the city. The goal? Improve human health. The result? Residents in newly greened neighborhoods showed 13-20 % less inflammation, a key indicator of cardiovascular risk, than residents in comparison neighborhoods. This local action crosses several benefits at once: Air through pollution interception, Climate through shade and carbon storage, Water through stormwater management, Biodiversity through habitat, and Equity because greening can be directed toward communities historically underserved by urban canopy., providing something rare: a measurable connection to Human Health.


  



   
   Air
	●
	Ultrafine particles and pollutants were mapped before and after planting. More than 8,000 trees and shrubs were sited to intercept traffic pollution along the Watterson Expressway corridor and selected to maximize pollution removal. Broader research links urban trees to filtering up to a third of fine particle pollution within 300 yards.

5. Local


5.1 Restoration Has an Address


Think globally, act locally. It’s a slogan people repeat without hearing. But restoration is not an abstraction. Habitats are restored one acre at a time. The first mile of a stream is cleared, then the next. Preventing a ton of topsoil from eroding starts in one field, then its neighbor. This work requires human intervention and succeeds at human scale. It happens in places with names and ZIP codes. The global crises we face are real, but they're not where the work gets done.


5.2 Cause and Effect


Global action asks people to trust that their contribution matters somewhere they will never go. The climate movement's figurehead is a bewildered polar bear on an ice floe. Most people will never see a polar bear, let alone one on an ice floe. They will never know what they did that put it there, or what they could do to change its fate. So they absolve themselves. The distance between cause and effect is too great to feel like responsibility.


Local action closes that distance. When a creek runs clearer or a hillside holds through a wet winter, the people who did the work can stand there and see it. That evidence compounds, building habits and traditions that carry not just across one season but over time, from one generation to the next. It’s the type of proof that turns one project into a second. 


5.3 Proximity Builds Trust


The hardest audience to reach is the one that has stopped listening. They will not be moved by another report, but they might be moved by a neighbor showing them something that worked three blocks away. Local work turns participants into the best advocates. They’re not asking anyone to believe, to change their mind, to get involved. They simply point at a result. 


Movement building is relationship marketing at scale. That works only because local organizations are answerable in a way distant ones aren’t. Their funders shop at the same grocery store. Their volunteers know whether the next promised planting actually happened. Their failures are visible before they can be reframed in an annual report. Proximity demands rigor and accountability in ways distance cannot reproduce.


5.4 Appropriate Scale that Scales Appropriately


Successful organizations reach an appropriate scale in one geography, then divide and begin again somewhere else. Their growth is usually imagined as vertical: bigger, taller, more uniform. With YES, scale is horizontal: from one to many. Leadership follows the same decentralized model. Successful local solutions become recipes: document the steps, adapt them to local conditions, substitute ingredients as necessary, then let the solution travel. RestoreSix makes that transfer possible: a creek cleanup in Portland and a seed planting in Sacramento become comparable, then repeatable. Local is not the alternative to scale. It’s the smallest unit.

6. Foundational Text: Molotov Alva and the Museum of Extraction


  

Molotov Alva and the Museum of Extraction is the foundational text of the YES Movement. Its narrative is built around these core principles: doing less bad is bad, nature thinks in wholes, the power of YES, taking local action, and the belief that ecological restoration can be defined and visualized using RestoreSix. The book is narrated by Molotov Alva, an avatar created by Douglas Gayeton for his 2007 HBO documentary. Nearly twenty years later, Alva returns to trace a path through 12,000 years of human extraction and the decisions and inventions that have produced today’s environmental chaos.


That journey leads to the Museum of Extraction. It’s both setting and metaphor: a collection of the ideas, inventions, and choices that transformed nature into a resource to be consumed. But rather than ending in human indifference and ecological ruin, the book proposes a way forward, one built on restoring the planet across six interconnected domains: air, water, soil, biodiversity, equity, and climate. Molotov calls this RestoreSix.


Can humanity stop being a force of extraction and become an agent of restoration? As the book closes, a global movement begins, uniting people and communities facing different challenges around a single word: YES. Scheduled for release in September 2026, the book will be released in a limited edition of 500 signed and numbered copies, available exclusively through the YES Movement website.


7. Community Building: The YES Movement’s Online Platform 




Environmental organizations seek funding to do their work and members to grow their movement. They also need storytelling that extends beyond their base and tools that express their impacts. The YES online platform provides all four.


How it works: An organization decides to plant natives along a local creek for erosion control. They consult the YES Field Guide for methods others have documented, or build a new approach from their own plans. They announce the project on the YES Action Map, carry out the work, then log the completed action with documentation. It then appears on the map tagged to biodiversity, soil, and water. Six months later, a group two counties over filters the map by the domains they care about, finds the first organization, studies what they did, then decides to replicate it in their own town. They announce their own action, gain members through the map, and do the work. Their version is documented in the YES Field Guide beside the original, where a third group finds it and starts again somewhere else.


Every step depends on the same thing: a common language and shared classifications for ecological restoration. Without it, the map is a directory, the Field Guide is a blog, and organization pages are profiles. RestoreSix is what makes them a system.




7.1 Restore Icons: The Restoration Taxonomy


[DESIGN NOTE: Two screens. First, show a wall of environmental icons. Second, show a detail of one icon along with its definition.]


Are we on the same page? Do we see things eye to eye? People reach agreements and organizations find alignment faster by first sharing a common language. Environmental groups face the same challenge. Habitat restoration means oyster beds to one group and prairie grass to another. One group logs its work as streambank planting. A similar group fifty miles away calls it native revegetation. A third writes it up as erosion control. All three did the same work on the same kind of creek, yet their terminology has conspired against them: none of them will ever find each other.


The Lexicon has spent years solving this challenge. Working with Google, Adobe, the Gates Foundation, and UN agencies, it developed Lex Icons, the world's largest peer-reviewed visual taxonomy for food, agriculture, and the environment. RestoreSix is built on this conceptual architecture. Taxonomies are more than a collection of terms or a glossary. They can embody consensus on the key terms and principles that define a group’s actions. That shared vocabulary is also what helps you find the right organization doing the right work, when and where you need it.








7.2 RestoreSix 


[DESIGN NOTE: Two screens. First, show a directory grid of organizations, the work they do, and the RestoreSix categories they focus on. Second, show a single organization with all six domains displayed, most lit up. The point is that one group's work registers across four domains when it is usually filed under one.]


The  restoration taxonomy is expressed through visualization tools. Environmental organizations are usually portrayed along a single dimension. When their work is depicted fractionally as being clean-water work, forest conservation, or endangered-species protection, it discounts the benefits they deliver everywhere else. A group pulling invasive species is filed under biodiversity, but the same work stabilizes soil, improves water retention, and reduces fire risk. RestoreSix helps organizations visualize their positive impacts as wholes rather than fractions, which makes them more attractive to funders and findable by prospective members.


7.3 Organizations


[DESIGN NOTE: Show a single organization and their RestoreSix ranking. Depict location, number of members, years in operation, numbers of actions on the map, RestoreSix focus areas where they work.]


Using the RestoreSix framework, organizations build a profile that shows their location, domains, current projects, logged actions, and impact to date, alongside videos, activist tools, and announcements of upcoming events. For most small organizations, this is the first impact record they’ve been able to produce without building the tool themselves. It is also where the map sends people, which makes it the first page a prospective member reads before deciding to join.


7.4 YES Action Map


[DESIGN NOTE: The  same organization is now on a map that features other organizations. The map is filterable by type of organization, RestoreSix focus area, number of members, and number of actions.]


Organizations post their completed work and announce what they're doing next on the real-time YES Action Map, filterable by region and by RestoreSix domain. Because every action carries the same tags, the map does something a listing can’t: it shows how a solution that starts in one place takes hold in another, so a group can see not just what’s happening near them but where an idea has already traveled.


The map is the platform’s most visible asset and its hardest to maintain. Organizations change, and some actions require local eyes to remain accurate. Two mechanisms help protect its integrity: partner organizations can correct and update their own entries, while new actions enter an approval queue where additional documentation can be requested before they appear.








7.5 YES Field Guide


[Two screens. The first is a searchable directory of actions, filterable by type of organization, region, and RestoreSix focus area. The second shows a single Action: a) the problem they faced; b) the solution they devised; c) the failures and successes encountered in implementation, treated as a recipe with ingredients; d) their success defined by RestoreSix impact areas; e) how they suggest scaling the action in other locations.]


In most cases, we don't have a finding the solution problem. We have a distributing the solution problem. Successful actions come from trial and error. From learning what works and what doesn't. And from refining the approach until it holds. Documented properly, this becomes a recipe: the steps are recorded, ingredients are substituted as local conditions require, and the solution travels. The YES Field Guide stores these recipes, and records what failed alongside what worked, because the approach that didn't hold is often the more useful thing to know. Entries are indexed by the benefits they produce. Actions are community-generated and expert-reviewed, and impacts are assessed through RestoreSix, which lets a group in one town trust a solution posted by a group in another.


7.6 YES Movement Building Toolkit


[DESIGN NOTE: A searchable resource library of tools and tips, including video tutorials created by members of the community.]


Knowing how to restore a watershed is not the same as knowing how to run the organization that does it. That knowledge gap is where most small organizations stall. The YES Activist Toolkit covers everything an organization needs to use the platform and run its own operation: explainers and onboarding guides for the platform itself, and tactical guidance on fundraising, audience building, and recruiting new members. Where the Field Guide documents how to do restoration work, the Toolkit documents how to do everything around it.

8. Communications Strategy: Substack and YouTube Channels
By design, the YES Movement meets people wherever they are. Online and in the real world. On the page. On the screen. On the street. The YES Online Platform is the movement’s core infrastructure, while Substack, YouTube, social media, changemakers, the book, and the Museum serve as acquisition and activation channels that bring people into it. Once on the platform, people can discover organizations, find opportunities to act, access tools and recipes, and see the impacts of their participation.


8.1. YES Content Strategy


The Lexicon is fluent across multiple storytelling platforms. It produces award-winning television shows (PBS, BBC, HBO), books (Random House, HarperCollins), and has hosted over 2,000 exhibits across the US. The YES content strategy is not aspirational. It is simply the work we already do, on subjects this team knows deeply and has produced on for years. It draws on The Lexicon's global community of freelance writers, art directors, videographers, and UX designers, already well-versed in the material, which reduces ramp-up time and staffing costs. Additional storytelling formats will include:


* Impact Certificates and Online Courses
* Podcasts
* Articles and Interviews
* Social Media Ephemera


8.2 Substack as a Livestreaming Platform


As a live-streaming venue, it hosts the weekly YES Field Guide show. Guests are drawn from the YES community. We meet the people behind the work, learn about the challenges they’ve overcome, and find inspiration to either join their work or launch actions of our own. The live recording is later transformed into clips, articles, and case studies that are cross-published on everything from YouTube to LinkedIn, and even on the Yes Platform itself. Josh Koppel and Zeldavision will manage this content network as an external contractor.

Sample Editorial Calendar


Every successful organization moves people through a fundamental pathway: encountering the work, seeing its relevance, connecting with others doing it, taking action, assessing the impacts, then scaling what worked.


ENCOUNTER  →  LOCALIZE  →  CONNECT  →  ACT  →  ASSESS  →  SCALE   [REPEAT]


To help organizations build their movements, the YES Field Guide Show turns this pathway into a livestream/podcast series organized around six seasonal themes. Each two-month season features two episodes a week, on Tuesdays and Fridays. Every episode showcases one organization working primarily in a RestoreSix domain, rotating through air, water, soil, biodiversity, equity, and climate so each domain receives sixteen episodes over the year.


Each season is built around a single question. The RestoreSix rotation gives organizations and sponsors a predictable editorial calendar months in advance. Episodes share lessons from successful organizations, and the final season generates the stories that begin the cycle again.

	Inside a season


Episode 1 opens the season by framing a question. Episode 8 is a mid-season cross-domain episode, pairing two organizations from different domains working on the same problem. Episode 15 is the community episode, drawn from Field Guide entries posted during the season. Episode 16 closes the season and hands off to the next. The remaining twelve slots rotate twice through the RestoreSix domains. Air, water, soil, biodiversity, equity, and climate each receive twelve episodes over the year.


The episode format


A.  The problem statement. How we got here. The specific local history, not the global one.
B.  The solution. The recipe used to tackle it, and how it succeeded. Ingredients, steps, substitutions.
C.  The impact. Which RestoreSix domain the initiative moved, and by how much.
D.  From the fraction to the whole. The other RestoreSix domains touched by this intervention
E.  Scaling success. An invitation to the YES community, pointed at the Field Guide entry and the map pin.

8.3 Substack as Publishing Platform


Substack works for YES in two distinct modes. As a publishing platform, it turns the online community into a contributor pool. Organizations and activists who have already documented a solution in the Field Guide can write it up as a story, which brings new audiences to their organization’s work and gives YES a steady supply of original content it does not have to commission. Articles also align with the show's six seasons, so written and streaming material surface answers to the same questions at the same time.


8.4 Substack Partners and Audience Sharing 


Substack's recommendation network lets publications recommend one another to their readers, making the audience a shared asset. YES will build reciprocal relationships with environmental, food-system, and place-based publications whose readers already care about the work but have no way to act on it locally. Every referred reader arrives one click from a map filtered to their own ZIP code.


8.5 Cross-Publishing on Partner YouTube Channels


Episodes and selected clips are offered to partner organizations for their own YouTube channels, with attribution back to YES. A partner gets professionally produced video about their own work at no cost, and YES reaches an audience that already trusts the partner. The RestoreSix tagging travels with each segment, so a clip published anywhere remains connected to the organization’s domains within the YES ecosystem.


8.6 Social Distribution on LinkedIn and Instagram


Posts on LinkedIn carry the institutional case to funders, agencies, and corporate partners. These posts include impact data, framework explainers, and short form video. Instagram carries the visual case to the general public. It may feature imagery from the book and the exhibition, before-and-after restoration work, and the people doing it. Both point to the same destination, and both draw from material already generated by the show and Substack rather than requiring separately commissioned content.

9. Real World: The Museum of Extraction Experience


The Museum of Extraction is the YES Movement’s flagship real-world activation experience. Like the book, Substack, YouTube, and the movement’s changemaker network, it creates encounters designed to lead people into the YES Online Platform, where those encounters become opportunities for local action. The experience is right-sized by scale and budget, and optimized in two formats.


FORMAT A:  MOTHERSHIP INSTALLATION


The flagship real world gravitational center of the YES Movement, designed to be experienced in person. It presents a compelling account of what 12,000 years of extraction has done to the planet, then concludes with an evocative call to action (say YES!) personalized to where you live (local) and what you care about most (RestoreSix). 


9a.1 A Personalized Experience 


Before entering, visitors are invited to personalize their experience through a mobile app. After providing their age, ZIP code, and the RestoreSix ecological benefits they care most about, geobeacons and QR codes deliver personalized text and audio as they move through the exhibition. Two people standing before the same interactive tableau can have entirely different experiences with the material.


9a.2 Antecedents: How Did We Get Here? 


The visitor enters a swirling tunnel of visuals and sound, a virtual time machine offering a compressed journey through 12,000 years of extraction. It reveals how humanity gradually transformed nature into a resource to be consumed. Drawing heavily from the book’s imagery, the tunnel moves from early agriculture and industrialization through fossil fuels, synthetic chemistry, the addictive nature of mass consumption, and the economic engine driving our culture of disposability. The message is clear: today’s crises did not appear suddenly. They are the accumulated consequences of choices made over thousands of years.


9a.3 Reality Check: Where Are We Now? 


The central body of the museum is a cavernous space filled with massive backlit images. These halls depict the consequences of the extractive system the visitor has just traveled through: climate disruption, biodiversity loss, degraded soils, polluted air, water scarcity, and social inequity. Rather than presenting these as separate crises, the experience reveals them as symptoms of breakdown within one interconnected natural system. The museum challenges fractional thinking, exposes the limits of doing less bad, and makes the case for restoration. Stops along this part of the visitor’s journey include:


Nature Is Normal. A functioning natural world gradually gives way to degraded landscapes, polluted waters, and disappearing species, revealing humanity as the disruptive force.


Weaving Spiders Come Not Here. An interactive gallery of illustrious robber barons and industrialists recounts their exploits, revealing how extractive capitalism transformed shared natural resources into private wealth.


The Hall of Great Human Works. Monumental mines, dams, oil fields, clear-cuts, and machines inspire awe while exposing ecological destruction at industrial scale.


Disposable. A celebration of convenience becomes a landscape of waste, showing that everything we throw away ultimately goes somewhere.


Tales of Enlightened Extraction. The human cost of industrial progress comes into focus through families whose bodies bear the consequences of polluted air and unchecked development.


The Awesome Power of Mother Earth. Wildfires, floods, rising seas, and other catastrophes become immersive entertainment, exposing our tendency to consume disaster while ignoring its causes.


The Extinction Futures Market. A casino-like trading floor turns endangered species into financial assets, revealing the absurd endpoint of putting a price on nature.


Last Migrations. Holographic animals return as ghosts of lost ecosystems, transforming spectacle into grief and opening the door to restoration.


9a.4 Activation: What Happens Next? 


The exit experience shifts abruptly from diagnosis to possibility. Most museums inform and energize their visitors, yet fail to offer the next step: how to turn passion into action. But not the Museum of Extraction.


Leaving the central hall, visitors enter another tunnel. This one is filled with digital static and distorted, chaotic sounds, punctuated by computer beeps and bleats. Light abruptly gives way to darkness. From cacophony comes quiet, then a single voice speaks. Your operating system has malfunctioned, it intones quietly. The planet requests your immediate reboot. 


Emerging from the tunnel, visitors discover a message waiting in their app. The YES online platform is a matchmaker. It has translated the visitor’s age and ZIP code and RestoreSix interests into opportunities to engage with local organizations doing work they care about, in the place where they live. The visitor leaves not only knowing more, but with a pathway to follow: encounter, localize, connect, act, assess, and help successful ideas travel.

FORMAT B:  POP-UP EXHIBITS 


In most cases, people working at ground level already know what needs to be done and the resources required to do it. What they lack are members, funding, and a way to tell their story. They want people to know they aren’t alone and that their actions matter. Most importantly, they need to believe that global challenges are best addressed by starting in their own communities.


Douglas Gayeton’s last book, Local, grew out of his work with community farming groups from Walla Walla, Washington to Salina, Kansas. That work created an informal network that helped The Lexicon stage more than 2,000 pop-up exhibitions on sustainability across the United States, each organized with local curators. Collectively, those exhibitions reached an audience in the millions. The Museum of Extraction’s traveling pop-ups will use the same model. Locally curated. Place-based. Designed to turn a global story into local action.


9b.1 Pop-Ups


Traveling pop-ups are smaller, place-based versions of the Museum of Extraction designed to move easily from city to city. Lighter and less expensive than the mothership installation, each pop-up introduces the core concepts of RestoreSix while combining select exhibition elements with locally relevant stories from organizations working in the surrounding community. 


Because the format is modular and portable, the exhibition can travel by flatbed truck, with components housed in modified shipping containers. The show can be presented as a complete experience or broken into components designed for rapid deployment at high-traffic locations. Wherever it goes, each stop generates local participation, allowing the Museum of Extraction to travel farther, activate more communities, and extend the YES Movement.


9b.2 Locally Curated


Two months before the exhibit appears, the YES Movement team begins mapping the region’s ecosystem of environmental organizations. This preliminary outreach identifies the groups already taking action and the RestoreSix ecological benefits created by their work. The most active organizations are then convened and introduced to the Museum of Extraction, the YES Movement online platform, and the RestoreSix framework. An Action Map is created to chart their work, and participating organizations are invited to join the online platform. Together, these organizations form a local curatorial committee that helps adapt the exhibit to the community: its places, its challenges, its organizations, and its opportunities for action.


By the time the pop-up opens, it arrives as an immersive experience shaped and owned by the very people already doing the work.

10. Target Audiences


The YES Movement serves three audiences. Organizations provide opportunities to act. Changemakers bring passionate followers. The General Public brings participation. RestoreSix connects these contributions and their impacts through a common framework, and the YES online platform brings greater visibility to successful local actions, accelerating scale.


10.1 Environmental Organizations


Environmental organizations are the YES Movement’s delivery network. YES does not replace the organizations already doing restoration work. It gives them tools, visibility, members, knowledge, and a common framework for demonstrating their impacts. They provide the opportunities through which YES participants turn interest into action.


Consider a group removing invasive species. Funders may classify that work as biodiversity enhancement, even though the same action can stabilize soil, improve water retention, reduce fire risk, and strengthen ecosystem resilience. When an action is defined by a quarter of what it delivers, its value is underestimated and the funding it deserves often fails to follow. RestoreSix makes those connections visible at scale.


The YES Online Platform provides organizations with the movement’s core tools and infrastructure, while the Museum of Extraction, Field Guide Show, Substack, YouTube, and changemakers expand their reach and bring new participants to their work. The Action Map locates an organization’s work by geography, so people can find it. RestoreSix describes its ecological impacts across domains. The platform introduces them to prospective members, partners, funders, and peer organizations. The Field Guide then helps organizations recruit participants, launch actions, document results, and publish what worked. A successful local action becomes a recipe others can adapt, allowing solutions to travel horizontally from one community to the next.


In return, organizations supply the movement's most important resource: real work happening in real places. They log actions, document impacts, share what works, and welcome the members routed to them. That exchange is the platform's central value. Nothing downstream happens without it.


10.2 Changemakers


Changemakers have attention and reach. The YES platform directs their followers toward tangible actions. RestoreSix gives them credibility they cannot manufacture: referencing a framework used by multilateral development banks and international agricultural research centers is a different kind of endorsement than a hashtag, and it answers the common charge that their advocacy is more passionate than well-informed. 


The Field Guide Show gives them material to share, including interviews, actions, stories, and documented outcomes. It also gives changemakers opportunities to appear on the show themselves, expanding their own audience while introducing followers to the movement.


The Action Map addresses their hardest problem: not simply what to tell people to do, but where to do it. Changemakers can direct followers to specific organizations near where they live, with real actions and dates already on the calendar. YES provides the local opportunity waiting at the end of the link.


In return for these tools, changemakers deliver reach the YES Movement cannot buy, and ground-level legitimacy with people who will never read a business plan (like this one!).


10.3 The General Public


People have climate fatigue. They don’t want more explanations about why the planet is about to go up in flames. It's not that they don’t care. It’s that the problem is too massive. Too insurmountable. Too far away. They seek answers to a much simpler question: What can I do where I live? Instead of writing a monthly check to address abstract problems halfway across the globe, they want a Saturday where tons of plastic debris are removed to restore a local beach. Improving the place they call home.


The Field Guide livestream/podcast show offers one starting point. In Year 1, it will feature nearly 100 organizations across the globe developing practical actions at the local level, not just addressing the climate crisis, but working across all RestoreSix domains. The show educates by example, offering recipes for solutions that can be adapted someplace else.


The YES online platform turns that interest into an address. While the Field Guide provides ideas and insights, the Action Map asks for a ZIP code. The crisis goes from happening everywhere to happening at a place they can drive to on Saturday. The online platform also gives people a reason to stay after the first action, showing what their participation accomplished. Along the way, they learn what else is happening nearby and where they can contribute next.


The public supplies the movement with participation. They show up and take part. When they document what happened and turn individual actions into visible collective impact, that story brings the next person in. This is how YES works. Movements begin when concern turns into action. 


10.4 Audience Acquisition


The Lexicon is highly skilled in mobilization and movement building. It created Reawakened, the first global collective action to safeguard agrobiodiversity, bringing together hundreds of organizations. The work crystallized at the 2nd International Congress on Agrobiodiversity in Kunming, China, where its Ten Principles for Agrobiodiversity were ratified by a delegation of international experts. 


This work led to Can You Change the Future?, an initiative funded by Google. It brought together 1,000 companies, UN agencies, and NGOs under NDA to develop innovative solutions across fields ranging from Food is Medicine and regenerative agriculture to single-use plastics and dietary guidelines. That work resulted in new nonprofits, open-source tools for sustainable food sourcing, and the foundation that eventually became RestoreSix.


The YES Movement builds on this expertise. The Lexicon already knows how to convene organizations around a shared framework, turn complex issues into consensus-led programs, and move large networks from conversation to collective action. YES applies that model at a far greater scale, connecting organizations, changemakers, and the public in a single system designed to convert attention into participation.

Phase III distributes production, programming, and governance across the network.

17. Organization and Governance


17.1  Legal and Fiscal Structure


The YES Movement is a not-for-profit program fiscally sponsored by The Lexicon, a twenty-year-old 501(c)(3) organization funded through private donations with support from family offices, foundations, government grants, and select corporate sponsors. The Lexicon fundraises on behalf of the program, administers funds, and manages all financial logistics.


Practical implication. Donations are tax-deductible from day one and the program carries no separate incorporation, audit, or 990 burden during its startup phase. The plan assumes fiscal sponsorship for years one through three, with a formal review at the end of year three to determine whether independent 501(c)(3) incorporation is warranted.


The Lexicon's prior work produced RestoreSix, an ecological benefits framework for planetary restoration covering air, water, soil, biodiversity, equity, and climate. RestoreSix is the guiding intellectual framework of the YES Movement and the taxonomy underlying all measurement.


17.2  Leadership


Founding co-directors Douglas Gayeton and Reuben Steiger share decision-making authority across fundraising, staffing, budget, scheduling, and external messaging.


Deadlock provision. Co-directorships require an explicit tiebreaker. Where the co-directors cannot reach agreement, the matter escalates to The Lexicon's executive director.


17.3  Advisory Board and Governance Pathway


An advisory board of twelve members will be seated in year one. It meets quarterly, holds no fiduciary authority, which rests with The Lexicon and is appropriate under fiscal sponsorship, and serves to pressure-test strategy and open funding and partnership doors. Two seats each go to environmental organization leaders representing the primary user base, environmental litigators, technologists with AI and measurement expertise, museum or immersive experience practitioners, philanthropic sector representatives, and movement-building practitioners from outside the environmental sector.


If the year-three review recommends independent incorporation, a governing board must exist before the transition rather than after it.

17.4  Funding Restrictions


Because this is a project about extraction, the YES Movement will not accept funds from extractive industries or from companies that derive material income from extractive activities.


The organization will adopt a written gift acceptance policy in year one that defines extractive with a specific revenue threshold. For example, any entity deriving more than five percent of revenue from fossil fuel extraction, mining, or industrial forestry can neither donate to the YES Movement nor be a recipient of work products it creates. Without this bright-line test, commitments will be litigated case by case at the worst possible moments. The policy should also address diversified holding companies, donor-advised funds that obscure origin, and foundations with endowments invested in extractive industries.


17.5  Data Governance and Privacy


The YES Movement collects personal information from museum visitors, platform users, and partner organizations. Because the project is a critique of extraction, extracting value from user data without reciprocity would be a self-inflicted wound. The governing principle is that the organization collects the minimum required to route a person to local action, and that partner organizations own the data they contribute.


* Data is never sold, rented, or licensed to third parties for any purpose
* Collection is minimized by design: age band rather than date of birth, ZIP code rather than street address, no persistent device identifiers in the museum app
* Partner organizations own the data they contribute, may export it in full at any time, and may delete it on request
*  No user or organization content is used to train third-party AI models without separate, specific, opt-in consent
* Visitor session data from the museum is aggregated or deleted within 90 days unless the visitor creates an account

	Minors. The Museum experience is designed to work for all ages, which means minors will move through it. No account is created for anyone under 13; under-13 visitors use a non-registered mode that personalizes in-session and retains nothing afterward. That is a COPPA requirement, not a design preference, and the experience cannot function without it. Visitors aged 13 to 16 require a verifiable parental consent step before an account is created.


Vendor agreements. Every vendor that touches personal data, including Allen AI, hosting, authentication, mapping, and analytics providers, requires a written data processing agreement specifying permitted use, retention, subprocessors, breach notification timelines, and deletion on termination. The Allen AI agreement in particular must state that user and organization content is not used to train models outside the YES Movement's own systems without separate opt-in consent.
Regulatory scope:


* CCPA and CPRA apply given expected California user volume: honor access, correction, and deletion requests within statutory windows
* COPPA applies to any user under 13
* GDPR applies if the platform is accessible to EU residents, which it will be; a lawful basis and a data processing record are required
* State biometric statutes are not triggered so long as the museum app avoids facial recognition and voice identification, which it should


17.6  Equity, Justice, and Community Accountability


A project documenting 12,000 years of extraction must be held to a higher standard than most. Equity is one of the six RestoreSix domains, and an organization that measures equity in others must be able to describe how it practices equity itself.


Reciprocity by default. Any community that hosts a satellite deployment receives, at minimum, free access for local residents, a full export of the local data generated, co-credit on any content produced, and a named local partner organization with approval rights over how its story is told.


Paid participation. Community advisors, local subject-matter contributors, and frontline storytellers are compensated at professional rates. Unpaid advisory participation is not requested from people whose expertise comes from lived experience.


Site selection is not purely fundraising-led. Satellite locations are chosen against published criteria that include cumulative pollution burden and organizational capacity, not only the availability of a local funder. At least half of satellite deployments in any two-year period are in communities meeting a defined environmental justice screening threshold.


Governance representation. A minimum of three of the twelve advisory board seats are held by people from frontline or environmental justice communities, drawn from the categories in Section 17.3 rather than added as a separate token category.


Language access. The platform and museum app support English and Spanish at launch. Additional languages   delivered by AI tools will be  added according to the demographics of satellite regions.

17.8  Content Moderation and Community Standards


Partner organizations post publicly to the Action Map, the resource library, and the Field Guide. A platform that hosts advocacy content near active litigation and local political fights needs published standards and a process, established before the first dispute rather than during it.


* No unsubstantiated factual claims about named individuals or companies. Claims about an identified party require a citable source
*  No content advocating unlawful action, property destruction, or trespass, regardless of the cause
*  No harassment, targeting of individuals, or publication of private information
*  No commercial promotion unrelated to a logged action
* Content concerning pending litigation carries a standard disclaimer and avoids assertions of fact about matters before a court


17.9  Political Activity


As a fiscally sponsored 501(c)(3) program, the platform cannot support or oppose candidates for office. Issue advocacy and non-partisan policy work are permitted within lobbying limits. The platform will not host candidate endorsements, and organization profiles that engage in electoral work must keep that activity off the platform. This constraint belongs in the partner agreement rather than being discovered by a partner mid-cycle.
